How ants find food without a leader

No ant knows the map and nobody is in charge, yet the colony finds short paths to food. Here is how, with a simulation you can play with.

Asad Razzaq, PhD4 Nov 20267 min read

Leave a crumb near an ant nest and within minutes there is a line of ants running between the crumb and the nest. Nobody planned that line. No ant has a map, there is no foreman, and each ant only knows what is right in front of its antennae. The line comes from a few simple rules followed by many ants at once.

That is swarm intelligence in one picture, and it is where my own field, swarm robotics, starts. Below is a small colony you can play with.

Three rules, no plan

Every ant in the simulation follows the same three rules:

  1. Walk forward, wiggling a little at random.
  2. Smell the ground just ahead, ahead-left and ahead-right. If you are looking for food, turn towards the strongest food scent. If you are carrying food, turn towards the strongest home scent.
  3. Leave scent behind you: home scent while searching, food scent while carrying. The longer it has been since you left the nest or the food, the weaker the scent you lay.

The world adds one more rule: scent slowly evaporates.

Notice what is missing. There is no rule that says “find the shortest path”, and the path is not stored in any ant. It is stored in the ground.

The ground is the memory: stigmergy

The French biologist Pierre-Paul Grassé coined the word stigmergy in 1959, while studying how termites build their nests. It means coordination through traces left in the environment. One individual changes the world, and the change tells the next individual what to do.

You already know human examples. A footpath worn across a lawn gets used because it is visible, and it stays visible because it gets used. A shared document with a half-finished section invites the next person to finish it.

For ants the trace is a pheromone, a chemical they lay and smell. In the simulation it is just a number stored in each grid cell, but the effect is the same: a good route makes itself more attractive.

Why the short path wins

In a well-known experiment, Goss, Aron, Deneubourg and Pasteels gave Argentine ants a bridge with two branches of different lengths between the nest and a food source. After a few minutes most of the traffic used the shorter branch.

No ant measured anything. Ants on the short branch simply finish their round trips sooner, so the short branch gets fresh pheromone more often. A small early lead attracts more ants, who lay more pheromone, who attract more ants. Biologists call this positive feedback.

Evaporation is the brake. A trail that is not refreshed fades away. That is how the colony forgets food that has run out. Watch the demo: the nearer pile usually gets the strong trail first, and when it is empty its trail fades and the colony moves on to the other pile.

Three things to try
  • Trail memory: Long. Ants keep returning to an empty spot because the old trail lingers. Too much memory means acting on old information.
  • Trail memory: Short. Trails fade before they can guide anyone. Too little memory means no learning at all.
  • Untick “Ants follow trails”. In five runs of this model, blind ants brought home about a third as much food in the same time (267 units against 740 over 2,000 steps).

The balance between using what you know and still exploring appears in almost every learning system, from ant colonies to reinforcement learning. Here it is a single slider.

From ants to algorithms

In the early 1990s Marco Dorigo turned this behaviour into an optimisation method called Ant System. Virtual ants build candidate solutions to a problem, such as the shortest tour through a set of cities, and leave virtual pheromone on the choices that belonged to good solutions. Over many rounds the pheromone concentrates on good choices, and the colony converges.

That idea grew into a family of methods called ant colony optimization (ACO). It has been applied to vehicle routing, scheduling and routing in communication networks, among other problems.

From ants to robots

Swarm robotics borrows the design, not the chemistry: many simple robots, each sensing only its surroundings, with no central controller. Three benefits make this attractive:

  • Robustness. If one robot fails, the rest carry on.
  • Scalability. The same rules work for ten robots or a thousand.
  • Flexibility. The group adapts to the situation without being reprogrammed.

In 2014 a Harvard team showed 1,024 small Kilobots assembling themselves into shapes using only local rules.

Robots rarely lay real chemicals. Researchers use stand-ins instead: light drawn on the floor, marks on a shared digital map, or messages passed from neighbour to neighbour.

The hard part is going in the other direction. With ants we see the rules and watch the trail appear. With robots we know the behaviour we want and have to find local rules that produce it. That inverse problem is why I use evolution to search for robot controllers, which you can watch in action in Watch a genetic algorithm teach robots to drive.

  • No ant knows the route. The route is stored in the environment as pheromone.
  • Positive feedback finds good paths; evaporation forgets bad and outdated ones.
  • Ant colony optimization turns this into a general method for hard combinatorial problems.
  • Swarm robotics uses the same principle: simple local rules, many agents, no leader.
