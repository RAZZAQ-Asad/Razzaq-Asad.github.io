Can robots smell? How a robot tracks a gas leak to its source

“Follow the smell to where it is strongest” sounds easy. In moving air it mostly fails. Here is why, and what moths do instead.

Asad Razzaq, PhD4 Nov 20267 min read

Imagine a gas leak in a large factory. You send in a robot with a chemical sensor and give it a simple instruction: go where the smell is strongest. It sounds like it should work. In most real situations it does not.

Smell does not spread the way you think

We picture a smell as a smooth cloud that is strongest at the source and fades evenly with distance. That picture holds in perfectly still air, or at the tiny scale of a bacterium.

In a room with any air movement, wind and turbulence tear the odour into filaments and patches. A sensor in the plume reads a sudden whiff, then nothing, then another whiff. Most of the time the reading is zero, even quite close to the source. At any moment the “gradient” points in a random direction, or nowhere at all.

Three ways to search

Random walk

The robot wanders and hopes. It is a useful baseline: any strategy worth having should beat it.

Follow the gradient

The robot has two “nostrils”, one on the left and one on the right, and turns towards the side that smells stronger. Biologists call this chemotaxis. It is how bacteria swim towards food, and in calm air it is fast and direct.

Surge and cast

This is what male moths do when they track a female's pheromone over long distances:

  • Surge. When the moth smells the pheromone, it flies straight upwind.
  • Cast. When it loses the scent, it stops advancing and zigzags across the wind in wider and wider sweeps until it catches the plume again.

The key ingredient is not a better nose but a second sense: wind direction. The robot in the demo gets an anemometer (a wind sensor) for this.

What happened in my runs

I ran each strategy 30 times in each air condition, with a limit of 4,000 time steps per search:

AirRandom walkFollow the gradientSurge and cast
Calm17 / 3030 / 30, avg 417 steps30 / 30, avg 478 steps
Breezy16 / 307 / 3030 / 30, avg 933 steps
Gusty19 / 301 / 3029 / 30, avg 1,536 steps

Two results stand out:

  • Following the gradient is the fastest method in calm air and worse than random once the air moves. Each whiff pulls the robot sideways, and between whiffs it has nothing to go on.
  • Surge and cast is slower in calm air but almost never fails. Its strength is robustness, not speed.

Press the race button and you will get somewhat different numbers, because it uses 20 different random searches. The pattern holds.

This is a recurring theme in robotics: the best strategy depends on the environment. Robust strategies use more than one source of information.

Real robot noses

Real gas sensors make the problem harder than the demo. The common metal-oxide sensors used in electronic noses take seconds to respond and longer to recover, so a whiff smears into a slow bump. A robot moving at walking speed has passed the patch by the time the sensor notices it.

Researchers fight back with better sensors, with probabilistic strategies such as infotaxis (move to wherever you expect to learn the most about the source position), and with teams of robots that spread out across the plume and share their detections.

That last idea is why olfaction interests me. A search that is hopeless for one robot in gusty air becomes far easier for a swarm that can cover the width of a plume at once. Odour search with multiple robots is one of the directions in my research plan.

The applications are practical ones: finding gas leaks in plants and pipelines, monitoring air quality, and searching collapsed buildings, where people cannot safely go first.

  • In moving air, odour arrives as intermittent patches, not as a smooth gradient.
  • Gradient-following works in calm air and fails in turbulence.
  • Moth-inspired surge and cast uses wind direction and stays reliable.
  • Several robots sharing their detections are a promising way to search turbulent plumes.
