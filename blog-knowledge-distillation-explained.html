Knowledge distillation, explained with robots

A small “student” network learns by copying bigger or more specialised “teachers”. Watch one learn three behaviours at once, and see why it matters for robots.

Asad Razzaq, PhD4 Nov 20266 min read

Big neural networks are good at their jobs, but they are slow and expensive to run. Some jobs also need several networks, each a specialist at one thing. Knowledge distillation deals with both problems. A smaller student network is trained to imitate one or more teacher networks, and ends up doing their work at a fraction of the cost.

Learning by imitation

The usual way to train a network is to show it examples with correct answers. Distillation changes the target: instead of the correct answer, the student copies whatever the teacher outputs.

That sounds like a step backwards, but the teacher's outputs carry more information than bare labels. An image classifier that says “this is a 2, but it looks a bit like a 7” is telling the student something about handwriting that the label “2” alone does not. Geoffrey Hinton and colleagues popularised the term in 2015, building on earlier work on compressing large models into small ones.

Distillation also works with several teachers. In policy distillation, several networks that each control an agent in a different task are merged into one network that can do all of them.

Try it: three teachers, one student

Below, three teachers each map a sensor reading to a steering command:

  • Go to goal steers towards the goal.
  • Avoid obstacle swerves away from things directly ahead.
  • Stay together keeps the robot near its neighbours.

The student sees the same reading plus which task is active, and it is trained only to match the teachers' outputs.

Size matters, up to a point. Here is the imitation error after 6,000 training steps in my runs:

A good student needs enough capacity to hold what the teachers know, and no more than that.

Why robot swarms need this

When you evolve controllers for a swarm (see the genetic algorithm post), evolution tends to produce specialists. A controller evolved for cooperative transport is good at transport and not much else. Real missions mix tasks: carry this, avoid that, keep the group together.

Evolving one controller for everything at once is hard. The tasks compete for the same weights, and the search space grows.

In my ANTS 2026 paper (Springer LNCS), I took the distillation route:

  1. Start from task-specific teacher controllers for transport, obstacle avoidance and aggregation.
  2. Distil them into one compact student controller.

The single student handles composite tasks that combine all three. A small controller also matters in practice, because swarm robots are small and cheap and have little computing power to spare.

The demo above is a one-dimensional cartoon of that idea. The paper does the same thing with neural controllers running in multi-robot simulations.

  • Knowledge distillation trains a student network to imitate one or more teachers.
  • Teacher outputs carry richer information than plain labels.
  • Several specialist teachers can be merged into one versatile student.
  • For robots, this gives small, multi-skilled controllers that fit on cheap hardware.
