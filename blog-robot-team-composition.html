Picking a playing XI for robots: how many scouts does a swarm need?

A cricket captain picks a different XI for a turning pitch than for a green one. Teams of robots need the same thinking, and the right mix keeps changing.

Asad Razzaq, PhD4 Nov 20267 min read

I play cricket on weekends, and before every match the captain makes the same decision: who plays? On a dry pitch that turns, you want an extra spinner. On a green pitch under clouds, an extra seamer. There is no single best XI. The best team depends on the conditions.

My PhD was about the same question for robots. If you can build a team from different kinds of robots, how many of each should you send?

Identical robots or different robots?

Most swarm robotics research uses identical robots. That keeps things simple, and a swarm of identical robots is robust because any robot can replace any other. Real tasks often need abilities that no single cheap robot has, though: seeing far, carrying heavy things, flying above the ground.

A swarm built from different robot types is called a heterogeneous swarm. A well-known example is the Swarmanoid project, which combined ground robots, climbing robots that could grasp objects, and flying robots that could see from above.

Mixing types raises a new design question that identical swarms never face: team composition. How many of each type should the team have?

Ten robots, two kinds

The simulation below has a team of ten robots and ten items scattered around an arena. The job is to bring every item to the base in the middle.

  • Scouts (teal) are fast and see far, but they cannot carry anything.
  • Carriers (amber) are slow and short-sighted, but each can carry one item home.

When anyone spots an item it turns red and goes on a shared map. Free carriers then head for the nearest known item.

What the simulation shows

Start with the extremes.

  • No scouts. Carriers wander until they bump into items. They get there eventually, but slowly.
  • Ten scouts. They find every item within seconds and bring home nothing. It is a team of eleven bowlers.

The fastest team is always somewhere in between, and where exactly depends on the conditions:

  • In a small arena, carriers find items by themselves, so extra scouts are wasted places.
  • In a large arena, wandering is expensive. Scouts earn their place, and the best line-up shifts towards more of them.
  • Clustered items change the answer again: once one item in a cluster is found, the others are nearby.

With scattered items, the best team in my runs had 3 scouts in the small arena, 5 in the medium one and 6 in the large one. The test uses the same 16 missions every time, so you should see the same result.

Because every mission is random, two runs of the same line-up can differ a lot. That is why the test averages 16 missions. Real experiments face the same problem, so swarm studies always report results over many runs.

Why this is a hard design problem

Two robot types and ten robots give only eleven possible teams, so we can simply test them all. Real teams are much larger:

  • More options. With four types and twenty robots there are 1,771 possible compositions. Each type may also have adjustable abilities, such as sensing range.
  • Behaviour depends on the team. The best behaviour for a carrier depends on how many scouts it works with. A carrier that waits for scouts to report is great in a team with many scouts and useless in a team with none.

Because of the second point, you cannot design the team first and the robots' behaviour afterwards. The two have to be designed together.

That is the core of my research. A genetic algorithm searches over the team composition, the robots' sensing abilities and their neural network controllers at the same time, for tasks such as cooperative transport and collective exploration. The papers are listed below, and there is a richer version of this simulation in the Swarm Lab. It has LiDAR scouts, pushing robots, a team budget, radio range and evolution.

What cricket gets right about robot teams

  • Specialists need each other. Eleven bowlers lose, and so do ten scouts.
  • Conditions pick the team. The best mix of robots changes with the environment, just as the XI changes with the pitch.
  • Roles and tactics go together. A team plan only works with the players who are actually in it.
  • Judge over many matches. One lucky run proves nothing; average over many.
