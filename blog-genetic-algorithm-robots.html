Watch a genetic algorithm teach robots to drive

Nobody programs these robots. They start with random brains and get better by selection, crossover and mutation. Then there is a course they cannot solve, and that is the best lesson of all.

Asad Razzaq, PhD4 Nov 20268 min read

Each of the 40 robots below has a tiny neural network for a brain: 56 numbers that turn what the robot senses into how fast each wheel turns. At the start those numbers are random, so the robots drive into walls or spin in circles. Nobody will correct them. A genetic algorithm will do all the work.

The recipe

A genetic algorithm copies the logic of natural selection. In this demo, one round looks like this:

  1. Population. Start with 40 random brains.
  2. Evaluate. Let every robot drive and give it a score, called its fitness. Reaching the target quickly scores highest. Otherwise, getting closer is better, and crashing costs points.
  3. Select. The best four brains are copied unchanged into the next generation (this is called elitism). The other parents are chosen by small tournaments: pick three at random and keep the best one.
  4. Crossover. A child takes each of its 56 numbers from one parent or the other.
  5. Mutate. A few numbers get a small random nudge.
  6. Repeat.

That is all of it. Nothing in the algorithm knows about wheels, walls or targets. It only knows that some brains scored higher than others. John Holland set out the theory in 1975, and the method has been used ever since, from antenna design to scheduling.

What the robots sense

Each robot has six inputs:

  • the direction to the target (two numbers);
  • three distance sensors pointing left, ahead and right;
  • the distance to the target.

These feed six hidden neurons and two outputs: the speed of the left wheel and the speed of the right wheel. To turn, the robot spins one wheel faster than the other, like a tank or a robot vacuum. This setup is called differential drive.

Things to try

1. The wall

Press Play and wait. For the first few generations most robots crash or drive straight into the wall. In my tests the first robot reached the target somewhere between generation 10 and 21, and soon after that most of the population followed.

Watch the chart. The best score (teal) jumps when someone discovers a new trick, and the average (amber) follows as the trick spreads.

2. Move the target

Once robots reach the target reliably, tap somewhere else to move it. Often many robots still find it. They did not memorise a route. They evolved a rule, something like “turn towards the target, and steer away when the side sensor sees a wall”. A rule works in places the robots never trained on, while a memorised route does not.

3. The mutation rate

The mutation rate controls how much each new generation changes.

  • At 2%, very little changes from one generation to the next, so progress is slow.
  • At 50%, children barely resemble their parents, and good solutions get destroyed as fast as they are found.

Like evaporation in the ant colony, it balances keeping what works against trying something new.

4. The trap

Now choose Trap. The target is above two walls, and the only route goes left, then right, then up. Run 50 generations. The algorithm will most likely get stuck with every robot pressed against the underside of the first wall, as close to the target as it can get in a straight line.

In my tests, one run out of four escaped the trap within 80 generations.

The real lesson: you get what you measure

The trap does not show that the algorithm is weak. It shows that the fitness score is wrong for this course. The score rewards being close to the target, but the only way out requires moving away from the target first. The score points the search into a dead end.

Researchers call this a deceptive fitness landscape. There are several ways out of it:

  • Better fitness design, such as measuring distance along the route instead of in a straight line.
  • Curricula, which train on easy courses first and harder ones later.
  • Novelty search, an algorithm that drops the objective entirely and rewards robots for doing something new. Joel Lehman and Kenneth Stanley showed that it can solve deceptive mazes like this one, where the objective-based search fails.

In practice, designing the fitness function is often the hardest part of evolutionary robotics. Everything the robots learn, they learn because the score asked for it.

Why evolve robot brains at all?

Evolving controllers like this is called evolutionary robotics, and it is a large part of my research. It has some clear advantages:

  • It needs no gradients, so it works with any simulator and any score, including scores that make no mathematical sense to differentiate.
  • It can score a whole team. In a swarm, fitness can be measured on the group as a whole, such as how fast the swarm moved an object, and evolution sorts out what each robot should do.
  • It can evolve more than behaviour. The same genome can encode a robot's body, sensors or, in my case, the composition of the team. See how many scouts a swarm needs.

The costs are real too. Evolution needs many trials, so it runs in simulation. Controllers evolved in simulation often behave differently on real robots, a problem known as the reality gap.

  • A genetic algorithm needs only a population, a fitness score, selection, crossover and mutation.
  • Evolved neural controllers can learn general rules, not just memorised paths.
  • Mutation rate trades stability against exploration.
  • A deceptive fitness score traps the search. Design the score with care.
